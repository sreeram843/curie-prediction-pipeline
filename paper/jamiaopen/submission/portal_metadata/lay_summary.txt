Hospital early-warning systems may interrupt clinicians so often that important warnings compete with routine ones. We studied whether the decision to interrupt can be separated from the calculation of a patient's risk score. Using public intensive-care records, we chose the interruption rules on one part of the dataset, locked them, and tested them once on the other part. A passive notification lane identified the same proportion of labeled patients as the raw score, while a stricter urgent lane produced about one eighth as many notifications. A quiet period after each notification and a rule reserving urgent notifications for stronger trajectories produced most of this reduction. The urgent lane still generated 1.30 notifications per patient-day and detected only about one third of labeled patients, so it is not ready for clinical use. The dataset's label partly depends on the same organ-dysfunction concept as the score, and the available data support only an incomplete SOFA score. The findings therefore concern alert routing, not diagnosis, treatment benefit, or patient outcomes.
