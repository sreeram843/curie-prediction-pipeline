\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Objectives:} A deterioration-score threshold may produce
repeated, poorly timed interruptions. We evaluated a frozen dual-lane governance
policy for partial SOFA, compared it with threshold-only partial SOFA and hourly
bedside scores, and attributed its effect to individual mechanisms.

\noindent\textbf{Materials and Methods:} We selected governance on PhysioNet
Challenge 2019 setA ($n=20{,}336$), froze it, and evaluated setB ($n=20{,}000$)
once. Detection required an emission within
$[\texttt{label\_start}-12\,\mathrm{h},+6\,\mathrm{h}]$; burden counted
interruptive emissions. Co-primary outcomes were detection/burden metrics and
normalized Challenge utility. Confidence intervals used 1,000 stay-level
bootstrap replicates.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} Governed partial SOFA matched threshold-only
sensitivity (79.5\%, 95\% CI 77.0--81.8). The interruptive lane emitted 13.2\%
as many alerts, detected 34.0\% of positive stays, produced 106.1 emissions per
detected positive stay (95\% CI 96.2--116.6), and had 10.7\% stay-level positive
predictive value. Normalized utility was $-0.111$, $-0.053$, and $0.041$ for
threshold-only, governed, and interruptive outputs, respectively. Removing the
90-minute refractory interval doubled volume; disabling the page gate increased
interruptive sensitivity to 44.6\%.

\noindent\textbf{Discussion:} A passive lane structurally preserved
threshold-crossing detection while refractory and page-gate rules reduced
interruptive emissions. Residual burden remained substantial.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion:} This single-resource, partial-score evaluation
supports further governance research but does not establish diagnosis, clinical
benefit, external validity, or readiness for patient care.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Lay summary}
Hospital early-warning systems may interrupt clinicians so often that important
warnings compete with routine ones. We studied whether the decision to
interrupt can be separated from the calculation of a patient's risk score.
Using public intensive-care records, we chose the interruption rules on one
part of the dataset, locked them, and tested them once on the other part. A
passive notification lane identified the same proportion of labeled patients
as the raw score, while a stricter urgent lane produced about one eighth as many
notifications. A quiet period after each notification and a rule reserving
urgent notifications for stronger trajectories produced most of this
reduction. The urgent lane still generated 1.30 notifications per patient-day
and detected only about one third of labeled patients, so it is not ready for
clinical use. The dataset's label partly depends on the same organ-dysfunction
concept as the score, and the available data support only an incomplete SOFA
score. The findings therefore concern alert routing, not diagnosis, treatment
benefit, or patient outcomes.

\section{Background and Significance}
\label{sec:background}
Electronic clinical decision support can generate technically correct but
operationally weak interruptions. Repeated exposure can reduce responsiveness,
and interruption cost depends on relevance and workflow fit rather than alert
count alone.\citep{embi2012,elias2019} Randomized sepsis-alert evaluations have
also produced mixed clinical results, showing that model discrimination does
not by itself determine the value of an alerting workflow.\citep{downing2019}

Retrospective deterioration studies commonly treat each positive score as an
alert. This collapses scoring, routing, and clinician interruption into one
event. We instead separate them. A deterministic scorer produces an auditable
signal; a shared governance layer then suppresses repetition or routes the
signal to a passive watch or interruptive lane. An optional language model may
explain an already-fired alert but cannot create, suppress, escalate, or alter
the score.

We evaluated this separation using the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology
Challenge 2019 public resource, which provides hourly intensive-care records
and an onset-aligned \texttt{SepsisLabel}.\citep{reyna2020ccm,reyna2019physionet}
Because the label incorporates a SOFA change, detection by a SOFA-derived score
is not an independent diagnostic validation. The estimand is the change in
retrospective alert-stream behavior caused by governance after the same score.

\section{Objectives}
The primary objective was to determine whether a policy selected on setA and
frozen before setB replay reduced page-eligible emissions while retaining
threshold-only partial-SOFA detection. Secondary objectives were to quantify
Challenge utility, compare the operating point with hourly SIRS, incomplete
NEWS2, and partial qSOFA, and identify which governance mechanisms produced the
observed changes.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{System boundary}
The Curie research prototype projects clinical events onto a canonical
envelope, transports them through Kafka, and applies versioned deterministic
rules in Apache Flink. Each score includes evidence identifiers, missing
components, rule version, and rule-content hash. The retrospective harness used
the same scoring and governance semantics but did not simulate delivery,
acknowledgement, or treatment (Figure~\ref{fig:system}).

\subsection{Dataset and split}
We used PhysioNet Challenge 2019 version 1.0.0 under CC BY 4.0. SetA contained
20,336 stays and setB 20,000 stays.\citep{reyna2020ccm,reyna2019physionet} SetA
was used for selection and setB as a locked retrospective holdout. SetB was not
used to tune or reselect the policy. This was neither an official Challenge
submission nor evaluation on the hidden Challenge test set, and setB is not an
external hospital cohort.

\subsection{Partial SOFA and threshold-only output}
SOFA summarizes dysfunction in six organ systems.\citep{vincent1996sofa}
Challenge records permitted an incomplete proxy reconstruction from oxygenation,
mean arterial pressure, platelets, bilirubin, and creatinine. GCS, urine-output
windows, vasopressor doses, and reliable ventilation status were unavailable.
Values were carried forward causally within each stay in the frozen replay;
future rows were never used. The respiratory proxy divided O2Sat (or SaO2 when
O2Sat was absent) by FiO$_2$ and applied the numeric P/F bands; this is not a
standard SOFA respiratory component or a validated S/F conversion. Other
available components used conventional SOFA cut points (Supplementary
Table~S1), missing components were explicit, and
at least two components were required. The threshold-only lane emitted at each
hour with partial SOFA $\geq2$. Thus neither a missing component nor an
unavailable organ system was assigned a reassuring zero.

\subsection{Governance and operating-point freeze}
The governed watch path required one qualifying crossing and no persistence or
baseline-delta gate. A 90-minute refractory interval suppressed repeated
emissions. The interruptive page gate additionally required two crossings,
30 minutes at page-level severity, a component-score increase of at least one,
and at least two positive components. Challenge rows are hourly, so the 30-minute
and two-crossing settings may be observationally redundant.

The original setA sweep evaluated 23 candidates under a legacy grace-6
detection definition. A candidate passed the sensitivity objective if governed
sensitivity was at least naive sensitivity minus 0.10 or at least 0.70, and
passed the burden objective if its interruptive-to-naive emission ratio was at
most 0.25. Candidates were ranked by passing both objectives, lower burden
ratio, lower emissions per detected positive stay, and higher governed
sensitivity. The selected candidate, \texttt{grid\_p0\_r90\_b0}, and complete
selection grid appear in Supplementary Table~S2. The configuration and resolved
rule bundle were frozen before setB evaluation.

\subsection{Comparators}
We independently scored hourly SIRS $\geq2$ using temperature, heart rate,
respiratory rate, and white-cell count;\citep{bone1992} NEWS2 $\geq5$ with the
unavailable consciousness/AVPU item assigned zero;\citep{rcp2017news2} and
partial qSOFA $\geq2$ using respiratory rate $\geq22$ and systolic blood pressure
$\leq100$, omitting GCS.\citep{seymour2016sepsis3} These threshold-only streams
had no refractory or page gate. The comparison is descriptive because missing
items disadvantage NEWS2 and qSOFA and none received matched governance.

\subsection{Outcomes and statistical analysis}
For positive stays, \texttt{SepsisLabel} starts approximately six hours before
the Challenge clinical-onset definition.\citep{reyna2020ccm} We call the first
positive hour \texttt{label\_start}, not bedside onset. Primary-window detection
required at least one emission in
$[\texttt{label\_start}-12\,\mathrm{h},\texttt{label\_start}+6\,\mathrm{h}]$.
Detection used any governed emission; burden used interruptive emissions.

We report sensitivity, total emissions, interruptive-to-naive emission ratio,
emissions per detected positive stay, alerts per patient-day, and stay-level
positive predictive value (PPV), defined as labeled positive stays detected in
the window divided by all stays with any emission. The PPV denominator is not
restricted to a matched onset window in negative stays. Lead time is from the
first in-window governed emission to \texttt{label\_start}; negative values are
after label start. Confidence intervals are percentile intervals from 1,000
stay-level bootstrap replicates (seed 42).

Normalized PhysioNet Challenge utility was co-primary and used the official
hourly weighting implementation. Positive predictions were emission hours only,
not latched states; consequently this is neither a leaderboard score nor a
utility estimate for delivered clinical pages. Drop-one ablations modified one
frozen mechanism at a time without reselection. Alternative timing definitions
were secondary analyses (Supplementary Table~S3).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Selection and holdout cohorts}
SetA contained 1,790 label-positive stays. Under the original legacy grace-6
selection estimand, the winner had 88.4\% governed sensitivity, 37.7\%
interruptive sensitivity, and a 0.123 interruptive-emission ratio. Applying the
later primary-window definition to the same winner yielded 87.6\% governed and
32.5\% interruptive sensitivity (Supplementary Table~S2); the distinction
prevents post hoc substitution of estimands.

SetB contained 1,142 positive and 18,858 negative stays and 31,749.8
patient-days. Threshold-only, governed, and interruptive outputs comprised
311,797, 159,933, and 41,158 emissions, respectively. Primary results are in
Table~\ref{tab:holdout}.

Governed and threshold-only sensitivity were both 79.5\% (908/1,142), a result
expected from a watch path that required one crossing and had no persistence or
baseline gate. Interruptive sensitivity was 34.0\% (388/1,142). The interruptive
lane emitted 13.2\% as many alerts as threshold-only scoring and 1.30 alerts per
patient-day, but still required 106.1 emissions per detected positive stay.
Among negative stays, 3,238/18,858 (17.2\%) had at least one interruptive
emission; their mean was 1.70 emissions per stay and median was 0 (IQR 0--0).
Stay-level interruptive PPV was 10.7\% (95\% CI 9.7--11.7).

Normalized Challenge utility was $-0.111$ for threshold-only emissions,
$-0.053$ for any governed emission, and $0.041$ for the interruptive lane. Thus
the most selective stream improved this retrospective utility relative to
inaction, while the other two remained below it.

Mean first-emission lead was 5.97 hours (95\% CI 5.54--6.39), but the distribution
was asymmetric: the 25th percentile was $-1$ hour, median 11 hours, and 75th
percentile 12 hours. A mean alone obscures that at least one quarter of detected
positive stays first emitted after \texttt{label\_start}.

\subsection{Comparators and ablations}
Incomplete NEWS2 and SIRS had 76.0\% and 78.5\% sensitivity but generated
ungoverned hourly streams requiring 163--213 emissions per detected positive
stay (Table~\ref{tab:comparators}; Figure~\ref{fig:burden}). Partial qSOFA was
quieter but detected 24.9\%. These results do not establish clinical superiority
because the scores were incomplete and their routing policies were unmatched.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The frozen policy reduced page-eligible emissions to 13.2\% of threshold-only
partial-SOFA volume. The refractory interval reduced repeated watch emissions,
and the page gate traded interruptive sensitivity for lower burden. Challenge
utility moved from negative for threshold-only and all governed emissions to
slightly positive for the interruptive stream. These findings support studying
alert routing as a versioned intervention distinct from the score.

Equal threshold-only and governed sensitivity is not an independent performance
gain. With one required watch crossing, no persistence, no baseline gate, and
no usable suppression flags in this dataset, any threshold crossing produced a
governed watch unless refractory timing moved it outside the analysis window.
The equality therefore mainly verifies that this routing configuration
preserved crossings. The empirical trade-off lies in the interruptive page gate.

Residual burden remained substantial: 1.30 interruptive emissions per
patient-day, 106 emissions per detected positive stay, and at least one
interruptive emission in 17.2\% of negative stays. Emission counts may overstate
or understate real interruption cost because replay had no episode arbitration,
delivery failures, acknowledgements, escalation, or clinician response. A
usable policy would need workflow-specific grouping and prospective burden
measurement.

The label-score relationship further limits interpretation. Challenge
\texttt{SepsisLabel} uses suspected infection and an acute SOFA increase, so a
SOFA-derived detector benefits from incorporation.\citep{reyna2020ccm} This
does not invalidate the within-score governance comparison, but it prevents
treating sensitivity as diagnostic validation. Comparator results are also
descriptive because NEWS2 and qSOFA were incomplete and ungoverned.

\section{Limitations}
First, this was one public resource with a competition proxy label; setB is not
external validation. Second, partial SOFA omitted major organ-system inputs,
used a nonstandard S/F respiratory proxy with P/F numeric bands, and used
causal carry-forward, which may not match bedside availability or
documentation latency. Third, the 23-candidate search and selection objectives
were specified within setA but were not registered prospectively. Fourth,
bootstrap intervals quantify resampling uncertainty within setB, not site or
temporal transportability. Fifth, outputs were offline emissions rather than
delivered clinical interruptions. We did not assess calibration, subgroups,
fairness, clinician response, treatment, or patient outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
In a frozen setA-to-setB replay, a dual-lane policy preserved partial-SOFA
threshold crossings on a passive lane while refractory and page-gate rules
reduced interruptive emissions. The improvement in Challenge utility and lower
volume justify further governance research, but residual burden, a partial
score, incorporation-prone labels, and absent external or prospective
validation preclude clinical-use claims.
